# 'Del' or 'Nabla' operator $\nabla$
the del operator is defined as the following *vector*:
$$
\vec\nabla = \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x} \vec{a_x} + \dfrac{\partial}{\partial y} \vec{a_y} + \dfrac{\partial}{\partial z} \vec{a_z}
$$
$\nabla$ is treated in every way as a normal vector with the difference that *partial derivatives* result with each operation on another vector with it
which will be more apparent in the next topic
## divergence 
a *dot operation* takes a **vector** and returns a **scalar**
$$
\vec\nabla \cdot \vec{v} = \dfrac{\partial v_x}{\partial x} + \dfrac{\partial v_y}{\partial y} + \dfrac{\partial v_z}{\partial z}
$$
notice the result isn't the sum of each vector component multiplied, rather the partial derivative of each component of $\vec{v}$
## gradient
takes a **scalar** and returns a **vector**
$$
\vec\nabla f = \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}\vec{a_x} + \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}\vec{a_y} + \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial z}\vec{a_z}
$$
*Notice*: the difference between a **scalar** and a **constant**
- constant: a *fixed value* that doesn't change, a number
- a scalar: something that is *NOT a vector*. it CAN be a constant of a variable or a function as long as can be described by just one value
## curl
takes a vector and returns another vector
$$
\nabla \times \vec{v} =
\begin{bmatrix}
	\vec{a_x} & \vec{a_y} & \vec{a_z} \\
	\partial/\partial x & \partial/\partial y & \partial/\partial z\\
	\vec{v_x} & \vec{v_y} & \vec{v_z}\\
\end{bmatrix}
$$
# divergence theorem
from: 
1. $\vec\nabla \cdot \vec{D}  = \dfrac{Q_{\text{en}}}{\Delta V} =\large\rho_v$    (**divergence equation**)
2. $\oint_\text{surface} \vec D \cdot \vec{dS} = Q = \iiint \rho_VdV$

we get: 
$$
\oint_\text{surface} \vec D \cdot \vec{dS} = \iiint \left(\vec\nabla \cdot \vec{D}\right)\,dV
$$
*IMPORTANT* the divergence equation above works on *ANY VECTOR FIELD* and not just the flux density $\vec D$ .
it can (and will) be used later on other vector fields that we will study

# divergence equation for each coordinate system:

## Cartesian
$$
\vec\nabla \cdot \vec{D} = \frac{\partial D_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial D_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial D_z}{\partial z}
$$
## Cylindrical
$$
\vec\nabla \cdot \vec{D} = \frac{1}{\rho} \frac{\partial}{\partial \rho} (\rho D_\rho) + \frac{1}{\rho} \frac{\partial D_\phi}{\partial \phi} + \frac{\partial D_z}{\partial z}
$$
## Spherical
$$
\vec\nabla \cdot \vec{D} = \frac{1}{r^2} \frac{\partial}{\partial r} (r^2 D_r) + \frac{1}{r \sin\theta} \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} (\sin\theta D_\theta) + \frac{1}{r \sin\theta} \frac{\partial D_\phi}{\partial \phi}
$$
# proof for the divergence equation

assume a very small cube with dimensions $\Delta x ,\Delta y ,\Delta z$ and volume: $\Delta V$ and point $P$ at its center with flux density
$$\vec D_p = D_{x_o}\vec a_x + D_{y_o}\vec a_y + D_{z_o}\vec a_z \qquad [\text{C/m}^2]$$

apply Gauss law
$$\oint_\text{surface} \vec D \cdot d\vec s = Q_{en}$$

We can expand this closed surface integral into the sum of six faces

$$\iint_\text{front} + \iint_\text{back} + \iint_\text{right} + \iint_\text{left} + \iint_\text{top} +\iint_\text{bottom} = Q_{en}$$
the flux density for the front face is equal
$$
	\iint_\text{front} \vec D \cdot \vec{\text{ds}} = D_{x\text{ front}} \Delta y \Delta z
$$
the value of $D_{x_\text{front}}$ can be approximated by:
$$
D_{x_\text{front}} =  D_{x_o} + \dfrac{\Delta x}{2} \dfrac{\partial D_x}{\partial x}
$$
- $\Delta x / 2$ the distance away from the point
- $\dfrac{\partial D_x}{\partial x}$  the rate of change of the flux over distance

substitute that in
$$
	\iint_\text{front} \vec D \cdot \vec{\text{ds}} = \left( D_{x_o} + \dfrac{\Delta x}{2}\dfrac{\partial D_x}{\partial x} \right) \Delta y \Delta z \qquad (i)
$$
for the back face
$$
\iint_\text{back} \vec D \cdot \vec{\text{ds}} = - D_{x\text{ back}} \Delta y \Delta z
$$
*negative sign comes from the face normal vector pointing back*

the value of $D_{x_\text{back}}$ can be approximated by:
$$
D_{x_\text{back}} =  D_{x_o} - \dfrac{\Delta x}{2} \dfrac{\partial D_x}{\partial x}
$$
substitute that in
$$\iint_\text{back} \vec D \cdot d\vec s = \left(- D_{x_o} + \dfrac{\Delta x}{2}\dfrac{\partial D_x}{\partial x} \right) \Delta y \Delta z \qquad \text{(ii)}$$

By adding the front and back faces from equations (i) and (ii), the constant terms cancel out to give the net flux along the x-axis.

$$\iint_\text{front} + \iint_\text{back} = \dfrac{\partial D_x}{\partial x} \Delta x \Delta y \Delta z \qquad(1)$$

Applying these exact same steps to the remaining faces 

$$\iint_\text{right} + \iint_\text{left} = \dfrac{\partial D_y}{\partial y} \Delta x \Delta y \Delta z \qquad(2)$$

$$\iint_\text{top} + \iint_\text{bottom} = \dfrac{\partial D_z}{\partial z} \Delta x \Delta y \Delta z \qquad(3)$$

Substitute the sums of all six faces back into the original Gauss's Law equation and factor out the lengths.

$$\left( \dfrac{\partial D_x}{\partial x} + \dfrac{\partial D_y}{\partial y} + \dfrac{\partial D_z}{\partial z} \right)\Delta x \Delta y \Delta z = Q_{en}$$
the left hand side is the definition of divergence and can be replaced with $\vec\nabla \cdot \vec D$
Since the differential volume $\Delta V$ is the product of $\Delta x \Delta y \Delta z$, we can divide both sides by it.

$$\vec\nabla \cdot \vec D = \dfrac{Q_{en}}{\Delta V}$$

Finally, recognizing that charge divided by volume is the volume charge density $\rho_v$, we get the divergence equation.

$$\vec\nabla \cdot \vec D = \rho_v$$
